AI GOVERNANCE / PERSPECTIVE

The AI control plane: governance that keeps up with deployment.

AI adoption is moving faster than most governance processes were designed to operate. The answer is not simply more policy. Organizations need an AI control plane: a practical operating model connecting use cases, risk classification, approvals, model providers, data, controls and evidence.

What changes

  • Move from annual policy review to live governance decisions
  • Classify AI by business impact and risk rather than technology label
  • Make model and use-case inventory operational
  • Connect security, privacy, legal and business accountability

What leaders should ask

  • Which AI decisions require escalation?
  • Who can approve high-impact use cases?
  • Can we prove which controls were applied?
  • How do third-party models change our risk?
CYBERSECURITY / EXECUTIVE BRIEF

Cyber resilience after prevention.

Prevention remains essential, but no credible security strategy can assume every control will hold. Resilience begins by defining the critical services the business must continue, understanding their technology and third-party dependencies, and designing recovery decisions before an incident.

Resilience means

  • Critical service mapping
  • Credible disruption scenarios
  • Identity and privileged access recovery
  • Third-party dependency visibility
  • Executive crisis decision readiness

Measure differently

  • Time to contain material exposure
  • Time to recover critical services
  • Quality of recovery evidence
  • Decision speed under uncertainty
  • Remediation of recurring failure modes
IT AUDIT / POINT OF VIEW

From annual audit to continuous assurance.

Technology changes continuously while assurance is often periodic. Better data, control telemetry and workflow evidence can make audit more current—but continuous assurance only works when independence, ownership and exception logic remain clear.

Where to start

  • Identify controls with reliable system evidence
  • Define meaningful risk thresholds
  • Separate management monitoring from independent assurance
  • Automate evidence collection before automating conclusions

Avoid

  • Dashboard theater without control accountability
  • Excessive metrics with no decision owner
  • False assurance from incomplete telemetry
  • Automation that obscures exceptions
LEADERSHIP / BOARD REPORTING

Board reporting should be a decision system, not a status pack.

The strongest cyber and technology risk reporting does not overwhelm leaders with vulnerabilities, controls or project updates. It connects business exposure, risk appetite, evidence, action ownership and the decisions leadership actually needs to make.

Board-level structure

  • Business services and material exposure
  • Risk appetite and threshold breaches
  • Control health and evidence confidence
  • Critical remediation and decision blockers

Questions to answer

  • What changed since the last meeting?
  • What could materially affect the business?
  • What decision is required now?
  • What evidence supports the conclusion?
AI LITERACY / EXECUTIVE PRIMER

What is AI, really—and why leadership needs a shared definition.

"AI" gets used as a single word for very different technologies with very different risk profiles. Machine learning finds patterns in historical data; generative AI produces new content—text, code, images, voice—from those patterns. Boards that treat both as one undifferentiated risk end up either over-governing low-risk automation or under-governing high-impact generative use cases. A shared, working definition is the first control.

Beyond the hype

  • Machine learning learns from data; generative AI creates new content from what it learned
  • "AI" spans statistical models, deep learning and large language models—each with different failure modes
  • Most enterprise AI risk today comes from data, integration and process, not the algorithm itself
  • Vendor tools increasingly embed AI whether or not it was procured as "an AI project"

Questions for the board

  • Where is AI already embedded in the tools we use, licensed or not?
  • Do we know which decisions AI is allowed to influence versus make outright?
  • Who owns accuracy, bias and explainability for each use case?
  • What happens when the model is wrong, and who is accountable for that outcome?
EMERGING THREAT / CYBERSECURITY

Deepfakes: when you can no longer trust what you see or hear.

Synthetic voice and video have moved from novelty to an active fraud and disinformation vector. Cloned executive voices have authorized real wire transfers; fabricated video calls have convinced finance teams to bypass approval controls. Detection technology consistently lags generation technology, which means the durable defense is procedural, not purely technical: verification habits that hold even when the audio and video look and sound completely real.

Why this matters now

  • Voice cloning has already been used to authorize fraudulent payments and wire transfers
  • Synthetic video and audio increasingly bypass traditional identity and approval checks
  • Generation tools are outpacing detection tools, so technical filters alone are not sufficient
  • Disinformation risk now extends to brand, leadership statements and market-moving claims

Practical defenses

  • Out-of-band verification for high-value payment, access and approval requests
  • Executive, finance and IT team awareness training on synthetic media tactics
  • Content provenance, watermarking and callback procedures where available
  • Incident playbooks specifically for synthetic media impersonation and fraud
FORESIGHT / RISK OUTLOOK

Emerging risks 2027: what leadership should be watching now.

The risks that dominate a board agenda in 2027 are largely visible today—if leadership is looking in the right places. Agentic AI, regulatory fragmentation, concentration in a handful of cloud and model providers, and the slow-burning exposure of long-lived encrypted data are not distant threats; they are current trends whose consequences compound. The organizations best positioned in two years are the ones building governance for these risks now, before they are forced to react.

Signals to track

  • Agentic AI systems taking action with progressively less human oversight
  • Regulatory fragmentation across the EU AI Act, US state laws and sector-specific rules
  • Quantum-readiness and crypto-agility for long-lived, highly sensitive encrypted data
  • Concentration risk in a small number of cloud, model and identity providers

Moves to make in 2026–2027

  • Build AI-specific scenarios into existing cyber incident response playbooks
  • Inventory long-lived sensitive data now as an input to crypto-agility planning
  • Stress-test vendor concentration, exit options and fourth-party dependency
  • Update board risk appetite statements to explicitly cover AI and AI-vendor dependency
